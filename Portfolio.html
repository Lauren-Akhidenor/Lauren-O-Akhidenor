A time-series analysis of UK food prices and fuel price shocks

Exploring how fuel price shocks feed through to UK food prices

Focus: Food Security | Affordability | Supply Chain Risk

Stack: Excel · Python (pandas, numpy, statsmodels) · ADF, Johansen cointegration, VECM, Granger causality, impulse response · Power BI, interactive HTML dashboard

  • Question: Fuel price shocks can pass through food supply chains, but how long it takes for consumers to see the effect is hard to pin down.
  • Action: Applied time-series methods to monthly UK fuel and Food CPI data (2015 to 2025) to test long-run co-movement, price transmission timing and how long the effects last.
  • What I found and learned: In this sample, found evidence of a long-run link between fuel and food prices, with fuel prices helping predict Food CPI at short lags (indicative) and food prices still adjusting after 24 months. Fish showed the strongest correlation with fuel prices. Built an interactive dashboard to explore the results.

Producing More, Losing More? A First Look at Agricultural Data

Exploring Production, Loss, and Trade Patterns Across Six Countries

Framework: Food Security | Availability | Supply Chain

Stack: Excel · SQL Server · SPSS · Python (pandas, Scikit-learn) · Power BI

  • Question: Food security is often discussed as a production problem, but production alone does not show how much food is lost or how exposed countries are to trade. I wanted to explore these patterns in FAOSTAT data.
  • Action: Prepared FAOSTAT data for six countries and four commodities using Excel and SQL Server, ran statistical checks in SPSS, trained a first Random Forest in Python to estimate production (five-country dataset, not yet validated as a forecast), and built a dashboard comparing production, loss rate, and trade exposure, with simple linear-trend projections to 2029.
  • What I found and learned: In this small sample, production rose in 15 of 24 country-commodity pairs, while food availability from the four commodities rose only about 3% between 2015 and 2023. Loss rates and export concentration looked worth investigating further, but the analysis does not show they are the cause. I also learned how to check a model’s evaluation, treat missing values carefully, and label estimated figures.

More Inputs, Less Profit

A cost–benefit analysis of organic and integrated farming for smallholder leafy vegetables

Framework: Food Sustainability | Availability | Profitability

Stack: Excel · CBA, Net Benefit Ratio, ROI, BCR · Sensitivity analysis

  • Question: Farmers are often told that more inputs mean a better harvest, but a bigger harvest only helps if it covers what it costs. I wanted to test whether adding fertiliser and pesticide to a manure-based system pays for itself when growing leafy vegetables.
  • Action: Costed two plots growing amaranth and corchorus, one using manure only and one adding fertiliser and pesticide. I compared net benefit, ROI and benefit–cost ratio in Excel, worked out break-even yields and prices, and stress-tested both systems against a 20% price drop and a 30% manure cost rise. All figures are built with live formulas in a six-sheet workbook.
  • What I found and learned: On expected yields, the manure-only system made a profit of ₦8,300 (ROI 19.9%), while the integrated system lost ₦9,450 (ROI −18.4%). The integrated system would need about 299kg, against 210kg expected, just to match the manure-only profit. The manure-only margin is thin, and a 20% price drop pushes it into a loss. The analysis can’t show why the integrated yield is lower, because yields are expected rather than measured, and the plots may differ. I also learned to work out every scenario before concluding, to distinguish break-even from “competitive”, and to state assumptions and limits clearly.
